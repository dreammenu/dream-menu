02 · Glasgow today

Glasgow orders generous, loud and familiar

What the city over-orders against the rest of the archive, and what the property market is saying.

Glasgow's table is generous, loud and familiar. The city indexes high on the margarita (the city's signature drink in the archive), scallops, tiramisu, fillet steak and chicken tenders, and low on the Negroni and the martini that define Edinburgh. That profile is a strength for the next five years: the forces below mostly reward classics done well, and they punish novelty that is only there to be photographed.

Market signalWhat it says about 2030
38 city-centre food and drink openings in 2025The most since 2018 and about 10% up on 2024, across 87,000 sq ft (Savills, February 2026). Demand for space is real.
Prime rents up about 15% to £50 per sq ftSpace is getting dearer where footfall is highest. Independents will look to the Southside and the East End.
Larger operators took about a quarter of dealsDishoom and Sticks'n'Sushi signal that national brands now see Glasgow as a must-have city. Expect more by 2028.
Rates rises of up to 900% for some barsScottish pub rates are forecast to rise 77% on average in 2026/27 (UKHospitality Scotland via Cater). Wet-led bars are most exposed.
More than four licensed sites closing a day across Britain98,914 sites at the end of 2025, down 382 in the quarter; casual dining down 1.8% (NielsenIQ, January 2026). Growth and closure are happening at once.

03 · The forces

Eight dated forces will shape the next five years

Wages, rates, the levy, weight-loss drugs and a generation drinking less, on one timeline.

So what for Glasgow venues

The visitor levy is the one force on this list that adds money rather than taking it away. From 2027 about £16m a year will be spent on the visitor economy. Venues that get into the events and marketing plans early will be the ones that benefit.

04 · The menu forecast

What Glasgow will order in 2027

Six quarters of demand, projected on the recent trend, with a planning range.

The archive covers the whole of Scotland, but Glasgow supplies most of it, so these lines are the closest thing to a Glasgow demand curve that exists. Three things stand out. The bar is moving from cocktails to spirits and whisky, quickly. Vegetables are taking space from chips on the side order. And seafood keeps climbing as a main course while steak holds its ground. None of this is a fad: each has run for at least four quarters. Treat the chips line with care, though: the first weeks of Q4 2026 point back up, and potatoes are seasonal, which is why its planning range is the widest on the page.

In

  • Whisky and spirits served simply, by the dram or in a highball
  • Vegetable sides treated as a dish, not a garnish
  • White fish, crab and shellfish mains
  • Ice cream and gelato as the default dessert

Holding

  • Steak, especially named cuts and sharing steaks
  • Tiramisu, the city's favourite dessert
  • Pasta and Italian
  • The margarita, even as the cocktail list shrinks

Out

  • Long novelty cocktail lists
  • Loaded and dirty fries
  • Chocolate-heavy desserts
  • Anything built for the camera first

06 · Scenarios

Three ways the city could go

A base case and two alternatives, with the early signs of each.

50%likelihood

Classics, done better

The base case: steady demand, tighter margins, a better bar.

Guests keep eating out but order fewer extras. Spirits, seafood and vegetables grow; cocktail lists shrink. The levy funds a few big events a year. The independent scene moves south and east as rents climb in the centre.

Early signs: spirits share keeps rising; set menus spread; new openings on Victoria Road and in Dennistoun.

25%likelihood

The events city

The upside: the levy and big events lift the whole year.

Levy money goes into a year-round events calendar. Hotel occupancy rises and visitors fill midweek tables. National brands keep arriving, and the city-centre vacancy rate falls.

Early signs: a published levy spending plan with events beyond the summer; hotel occupancy up two years running.

25%likelihood

The squeeze

The downside: costs outrun prices and the middle closes.

Rates, wages and weaker appetite (weight-loss drugs, less drinking) close mid-market casual dining and wet-led bars. The scene polarises between cheap and special.

Early signs: more than one closure a week in the city centre; spend per head falling for two surveys in a row.

The likelihoods are Dream Menu's judgement, not a model output. They exist to force a plan for each case: an operator who has a plan only for the base case is betting the business on a coin that lands heads half the time.

08 · Risks and signals

What would change our view

The signals to track over the next two quarters, and what each would mean for the recommendations.

Spirits share

If spirits stall below a quarter of dream drinks in 2027, the bar shift is slower than projected.

The levy spending plan

A plan weighted to year-round events moves the odds towards "The events city".

City-centre closures

More than one a week for a quarter would move the odds towards "The squeeze".

Southside rents

Rents rising faster than footfall would mean the district is pricing itself out, as Finnieston did.

Method and sources

How the projections work. Each line is a recency-weighted straight line through the quarters with enough answers, started from the latest solid quarter and damped by 30% each quarter so that no trend runs on for ever. The shaded band is a planning range that widens with the horizon and with the steepness of the trend, because a steep trend is the one most likely to stall. It shows how far the line could reasonably wander, not a statistical confidence interval. These are projections of the recent trend, not predictions: they say where things go if nothing changes, which is useful precisely because something usually does. Calls beyond 2027 are Dream Menu judgement, combining the projections with the published research cited.

City index: Glasgow's share of answers for each dish against the share across all answers, times 100. District figures use venues guests named in Glasgow with a known address, assigned to the nearest district centre within 1.6 km (Shawlands joins the Southside, Dennistoun the East End).

Dish categories were made more specific on 24 October 2026; each answer is coded to the dish itself with a parent group. A clear move means a two-proportion test between July to December 2025 and April 2026 onward came out at p < 0.05. Dream Menu is qualitative fieldwork with food-literate guests, chefs and hospitality staff, not a statistically representative survey of the population. How Dream Menu works.

Sources: Savills, February 2026, The Scotsman, Glasgow City Council, UKHospitality Scotland via Cater, NielsenIQ, January 2026, IGD via Restaurant Online, July 2026, The Boar, October 2026.
