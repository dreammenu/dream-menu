The top 20 operator actions

From foundations (1 to 5: identity, menu, digital basics) through operational levers (6 to 15) to strategy (16 to 20). Where a tool on this page helps, it is linked.

  1. Define one clear, sayable reason to visit

    If a guest cannot describe you in one sentence, they are less likely to recommend you.

  2. Identify and protect your signature dish or drink

    Find what people already talk about most and treat it as a protected asset.

  3. Cut the bottom end of your menu

    Long menus signal uncertainty. Short ones signal confidence and support margin. Find your weak dishes.

  4. Put one named supplier on the menu this week

    One real supplier with a real story says more than ten claims about quality.

  5. Update your online menu everywhere

    Website, Instagram link, Google and booking platforms should all show what you actually serve.

  6. Fix your Google Business profile

    Hours, menu link, current photos, an honest description. People on Google are close to deciding.

  7. Reply to your recent reviews properly

    Specific and warm. Never defensive, never silent. How to reply.

  8. Replace AI or over-produced imagery with honest photos

    Real food, shot honestly, in the actual room.

  9. Give every server one genuine recommendation

    One real recommendation from someone who has eaten the dish beats a script.

  10. Design a stronger ending

    Dessert, the bill and the goodbye are remembered far more than the middle of the meal. The last five minutes.

  11. Build a drinks programme that matters

    Drinks are one of the clearest margin levers. Take them as seriously as the food.

  12. Create one accessible entry point

    A bar snack, a set lunch, a roast. Honest and easy to commit to.

  13. Design Sunday as a full proposition

    Its own menu, booking mechanics, provenance and atmosphere. Sunday checklist.

  14. Track RevPASH and real session performance

    Booking data, covers by session, dish sales, drinks mix. Discipline, not complexity. Run the numbers.

  15. Stop carrying items that earn neither advocacy nor margin

    Every dish should build reputation, support margin, or both.

  16. Treat dessert as retained revenue

    A weak dessert hands the final spend and the final memory to someone else.

  17. Be explicit about the occasion you are built for

    Celebration, date, work lunch, treat, regular, walk-in, group.

  18. Invest in staff culture as a commercial system

    Retained people produce consistency and warmth guests notice.

  19. Choose your neighbourhood as carefully as your menu

    Site selection is a strategic skill, not just a property decision.

  20. Make big-event legacy count

    Keep the digital presence, group mechanics and visibility you build for events, and follow up first-time guests.

Your numbers

Each tool opens with example figures. Type over them with your own. Nothing you enter leaves your browser. Prices include VAT at 20% unless a box says otherwise; profit is worked out on the price before VAT.

1How healthy is the business?

RevPASH

Revenue per available seat hour: weekly revenue ÷ (seats × hours open). The number every operator should know.

Cost health check

Each line as a percentage of revenue (food cost as a share of food revenue).

2Pricing decisions

What should this dish cost?

From the cost of one plate to a menu price that hits your target gross profit, rounded to a clean price point.

Price-rise survival

How many covers you could lose before a price rise costs you money. Most operators overestimate how many guests will walk. 61% of the operators we surveyed had changed prices in the previous three to six months.

3Where profit leaks

No-show cost

What empty booked tables cost over a year. 11% of surveyed operators said no-shows and cancellations had got worse. One free fix.

Is this session worth opening?

Rent and rates are paid whether you open or not, so judge a quiet lunch or Monday on what it adds after staff and running costs.

Waste in pounds

Weigh or cost one normal day's bin, then see the year. The second figure is the one that changes behaviour.

Delivery: the true margin

Profit on one dish sold through a delivery app compared with the same dish in the room.

4What a guest is worth

The value of a regular

What one loyal guest is worth over the years they keep coming. Useful when deciding how generous to be.

Reading the results

  • Gross profit is what is left from sales after ingredients (and VAT). Staff, rent and energy come out of it.
  • Green means within the benchmark, amber worth watching, red act on it.
  • These are decision aids, not accounts. Use your own till and supplier figures where you have them.
  • Benchmarks below are for a quality independent. Pubs, cafés and hotel restaurants will differ.

Your menu

Menu matrix: which dishes earn their place

Paste one dish per line: name, number sold, menu price, ingredient cost. A month of till data is ideal. Each dish is sorted by how popular it is and how much it makes per plate, compared with the rest of your menu. You can paste straight from a spreadsheet.

Star Popular and profitable. Protect it, give it the best spot on the menu, never let it slip.

Workhorse Popular but thin. A small price rise, a cheaper garnish or a tighter portion.

Puzzle Profitable but few order it. Rename it, move it, get servers to recommend it.

Dog Neither. Cut it, or rework it completely.

Menu copy

  • Name suppliers: "Greenheart Growers asparagus", not "seasonal asparagus".
  • Describe technique, not adjectives: "slow-braised", "house-fermented", not "tender" or "delicious".
  • Name dishes plainly so they are quotable.
  • Allergens: clear and confident, without a defensive preamble.
  • One signature side or snack priced fairly can earn its own following.
  • A happy hour builds community and signals confidence.
  • Pricing psychology (anchors, bread, freebies) is covered in Odd ideas that work.
Behavioural ideas

Odd ideas that work

Guests do not judge a night out like an accountant. They judge how it felt: how long the wait seemed, whether they felt looked after, how it ended. Small changes to those feelings often beat expensive fixes to the operation. Some of these sound backwards. That is the point: if an idea were obviously sensible, your competitors would already be doing it.

New: 20 more ideas, tested against the research. An evidence map, effect sizes, a league table and a trial planner.See the tested ideas →
Test, don't argue. Pick one idea. Change only that. Run it on alternate weeks or alternate nights for a month and count the thing in "How you'll know". Keep what works, drop what doesn't, and tell us what happened.

Ask, don't tell

Usual view
"Please call us if you can't make it."
The bet
Ask a question the guest has to answer: "Will you let us know if your plans change?" People keep promises they make in their own words far better than they follow instructions.
Try this week
Change the wording on the phone, the booking confirmation and the reminder text. Cost: nothing.
How you'll know
No-shows per week, four weeks before and after. Put a value on it.

Make the wait feel shorter

Usual view
Speed up the kitchen.
The bet
Empty, unexplained waiting feels longest. The same twelve minutes feel short if there is something to eat, watch or know.
Try this week
Something on the table within five minutes of ordering. Tell each table when mains are a few minutes away.
How you'll know
Comments and reviews that mention "slow" or "waited".

Add a decoy

Usual view
Every dish has to sell.
The bet
One deliberately expensive item makes the next one down look fair. It doesn't need to sell much to do its job.
Try this week
Add a premium steak, sharing board or bottle above your current top price.
How you'll know
Sales of your second most expensive item and average spend per head. Check it in the matrix.

Give away what looks costly

Usual view
Charge for everything you can.
The bet
Tap water poured without asking, or a free top-up of tea, costs pennies and reads as generosity. In our debates 35% of 1,195 guests would choose tap water anyway, so make it a welcome, not a request.
Try this week
A jug of cold tap water on the table as guests sit down.
How you'll know
Drinks spend per head (check it holds) and mentions in reviews.

Charge for the bread

Usual view
Free bread is hospitality.
The bet
Free bread is taken for granted and fills people up before mains and dessert. Good bread, named and fairly priced, gets valued and talked about.
Try this week
A named house loaf with proper butter at a few pounds, offered not assumed.
How you'll know
Dessert orders, bread waste and mentions online.

Sell permission to indulge

Usual view
Comfort food is beneath a serious menu.
The bet
Takeaway pizza is the guilty pleasure guests name most (80 mentions), ahead of Pot Noodles and spag bol. People want a licence to indulge. A knowing, well-made "guilty" dish gives them one, and a story to tell.
Try this week
An off-menu or late-night guilty pleasure that servers mention by name.
How you'll know
How many sell, and how often it turns up on social.

Make quiet nights the product

Usual view
Fill quiet nights with discounts.
The bet
Some guests will pay full price for calm. Sell the quiet itself: music down, easy to talk, solo diners welcome.
Try this week
Call Tuesday "the quiet night" on Instagram and the website. Same prices.
How you'll know
Covers and spend against a normal Tuesday. Is the session worth it?

Drop a trend out loud

Usual view
Follow trends to stay relevant.
The bet
Matcha (111 mentions) and Dubai chocolate (105) top the list of trends guests would happily bin. Saying publicly that you've dropped something shows you listen, and stands out more than adding the next thing.
Try this week
Post why you've taken a tired item off, and what replaces it.
How you'll know
Comments, shares and whether anyone asks for it back.

Cook a guest's dream dish

Usual view
Specials come from the chef.
The bet
A special built from a real guest's dream menu, credited to them, is local, personal and made to be shared. Over 1,200 dream menus are there to draw on.
Try this week
Plan one credited special a month. Ask us for dream dishes from your area.
How you'll know
Special sales and shares of the guest's post.

Be a bit unreasonable

Usual view
Be consistent and sensible.
The bet
Sensible is forgettable. One slightly odd, generous habit becomes the thing people tell their friends about you.
Try this week
A handwritten note on birthday bills, a bell when someone orders the signature dish, a biscuit for every dog.
How you'll know
How often it's mentioned in reviews and posts.

Social media and reviews

The three-post week

  1. Monday or Tuesday, the dish: one honest photo in natural light, a short caption naming the dish and supplier.
  2. Thursday, the process or the place: prep, a supplier visit, the room. More intimate, first person.
  3. Weekend, the community: credit a guest's post, a staff recommendation, what's on this weekend.

Reels: one beautiful moment in 10 to 20 seconds, best frame first. TikTok: the process and the person, one take on a phone beats a produced video.

Content creators

The most valuable posts come from people who paid. "I went with my own money and it was the best thing I've eaten this year" is trusted far more than a gifted post. Make a venue worth posting about, then treat creators like any other guest.
