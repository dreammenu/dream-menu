Seven key findings

  1. Advocacy follows clarity

    The strongest venues have a clear identity and a reason to visit you can say in one sentence. This holds in both the community interviews and the operator survey.

  2. The dataset captures influence

    The 1,175 interviewees reach an estimated 4.4 million accounts between them. They are the people others listen to when deciding where to eat.

  3. Classics still win

    Seafood starters, steak, pasta, tiramisu, sticky toffee pudding, fries, martinis and margaritas dominate when done exceptionally well. Trend-chasing is actively tiring people.

  4. Place matters

    Advocacy concentrates in a few districts. The Southside and the Barras are the highest-opportunity emerging corridors.

  5. Brand execution is inconsistent

    Digital basics, photography, menus and review replies are a big untapped lever. AI-generated food imagery now actively damages trust.

  6. Operators are losing visibility, not volume

    Predictability over the next three months scores 3.0 out of 10. 89% of operators answering see supplier prices rising, and 80% name tax and rates relief as the one thing that would help most.

  7. Demand is moving, and it can be measured

    Five quarters of tracking now show which dishes and drinks are gaining share and which are fading. The full demand tracker is in the partner area and the report sections.

Top 5 opportunities

  1. Own a documented concept gap: late-night dessert, a city-centre Sunday roast, a quality gastropub, elevated Glasgow-Chinese comfort food, West African.
  2. Build around one memorable signature dish, drink or experience that travels on its own: the scallops, the focaccia, the miso old fashioned, the spiced fries.
  3. Go where community demand is ahead of quality supply: the Southside, the Barras, the East End, parts of Partick.
  4. Improve profit through simplicity, drinks and digital basics: short confident menus, a serious cocktail and no/low list, honest photography, an up-to-date Google profile, real review replies.
  5. Turn the Commonwealth Games into long-term growth: keep the group-booking mechanics, longer hours and visibility built for the Games, and bring first-time visitors back.

Top 5 risks

  1. Reading either dataset as the whole market. The interviews describe the food-literate audience, not every diner. The operator survey is directional.
  2. Following trends the community is already rejecting: Dubai chocolate and pistachio, matcha, AI imagery, gimmick presentation.
  3. Cost pressure in a hard year: food, energy, labour and rent each named by 52 to 63% of operators; 41% trading worse than a year ago.
  4. No clear identity or signature asset. The middle-market independent, squeezed between differentiated venues and chains, is where the pain concentrates.
  5. Treating the Games as an end point rather than a catalyst. Their value depends on turning first visits into regulars.

Five questions before you invest time or money

If a venue, concept or investment cannot answer all five clearly, the report suggests it is not ready yet.

1

Is there a clear reason to visit?

One sentence a guest could use to recommend you.

2

Is there a signature dish, drink or experience?

A named asset that travels in conversation on its own.

3

Are the digital basics in order?

Current menu everywhere, Google profile, honest photos, review replies.

4

Are the unit economics modelled, not assumed?

Covers, spend per head, RevPASH, drinks mix, dish margin.

5

Is the venue built for a specific occasion?

Celebration, treat, regular, group or work lunch.

About the report

Four original datasets: 1,175 community interviews with chefs, hospitality people, food writers and food-literate diners (September 2025 to October 2026); a 54-operator field survey (April to May 2026); 64 chef and owner interviews; and 52 Dream Fringe responses. 1,345 responses in all. Everything is stated preference, not sales data.
