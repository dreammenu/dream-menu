What the data says this quarter

Part two · What guests want

2How settled the menu is

 

Start with the shape of demand. Where guests agree on what a course should be, a menu has to deliver the favourite well. Where they do not, there is room to be different.

Use it todecide where to be different. Keep a settled course short and well made, and put your signature dish in the most open one.

3The dishes that hold

13 dishes hold their share in every quarter: the core of any menu

Most dishes drift up and down with the sample. A few never do. These are the staples guests keep choosing whatever else is in fashion, and beside them is how many of each course’s dishes are rising, steady or fading. Partners can open every dish by name in the reference charts.

Use it tomanage the range: protect the staples, trial rising dishes as specials, and retire fading ones on your own schedule.

Part three · What is changing

4What changed

Sides are being rewritten; the rest of the menu is holding

So much for what holds. Now for what moves. Comparing late 2025 with the recent quarters, 18.3% of side answers sit in a different food group, against about 8.0% that sampling alone would produce. No other course clears that bar. Do guests pile onto fewer favourites as a result? No: the five favourite dishes in each course hold the share they did a year ago (reference charts). Partners also see where the share went, food group by food group.

Use it toset priorities for the next menu change. Review sides first; change starters, mains and desserts for cost or quality, not to chase a trend.

5Drinks, restarted

What guests drink, on a clean new series

Drinks need their own telling. The question was split into soft and alcoholic on 23 August 2026, so the old drink trends stopped there. This is the new baseline, built only from the new questions; movement will be tested once two quarters each hold 100 answers.

Use it toset a baseline. Price alcohol-free properly now, and wait for two full quarters before acting on movement.

Part four · Where it differs

6Every city’s appetite

 

The national picture hides real differences. Shares, not counts, are used throughout, so a city with fewer interviews is compared fairly with Glasgow.

Use it tofit a menu to its market. Lean into dishes that sit above average in your city, and think twice before importing a favourite that sits below it.

7Guests and critics

 

Use it todecide what to promote. Where guests and critics agree, shout about it; where guests lead, you are ahead of the guides.

Part five · The reality check

8How we compare

Outside data agrees with Dream Menu on 3 topics and challenges it on 5

A panel of food-led people is not the general public, so every finding deserves a check. Here national polls, eating-out data, menu trackers and sales figures sit beside Dream Menu’s own numbers. Outside figures are never mixed into Dream Menu data: they stand alongside it, with their source and date, as a check and a challenge. Partners also see dish, drink and operator comparisons.

Use it tocalibrate confidence. Where outside data agrees, act; where it differs, test first, because foodies tend to lead the wider public.

Part six · What to do

9What to do about it

The same evidence, read for four kinds of reader

Each recommendation follows from the sections above and is rewritten when the numbers change.

Running a venue or a kitchen

Review the side list first: it is the only course that has moved beyond chance. Protect the staples (tiramisu, scallops and triple-cooked chips). Give alcohol-free its own priced section: 7.9% of guests choose it when asked for an alcoholic drink.

Opening a venue or investing

Guests broadly agree on what desserts should be, so compete there on quality, not novelty. Starters are the most open course: that is where a signature dish can stand out. Recommendation is concentrated (the top 10% of venues take 44% of mentions), so plan for word of mouth from day one.

Method

Who answers, and how these numbers are made

Who answers

Share of the 1,250 guests who answered the dish questions, by how they describe themselves.

iHow to read this

Each bar is one group’s share of the panel. Groups come from how guests introduce themselves, so they are a guide, not a strict job title.

The panel leans towards people who work in or write about food, which is why it tends to spot trends before the wider public.

Industry (chefs, operators, front of house)37%
Creators (food pages and writers)33%
Enthusiasts (people who love eating out)29%

Where their venues are

Share of the 3,011 UK venue mentions, by nation. 15% of all 3,555 mentions are outside the UK or not placed in a nation.

iHow to read this

Each bar is a nation’s share of the venues guests named in the UK. It shows where the evidence is strongest: findings lean towards Scotland because most venues named are there.

Scotland95%
England5%
Northern Ireland<1%
Wales<1%

Read it as: a large panel of food-led people, most of them in Scotland and many working in the trade. It leads the wider public on trends more than it mirrors it, which is why chapter 9 sets the findings against national sources. Age, home city and how often guests eat out are not collected yet.

  • Shares are of answers coded to a category within each course. Answers coded as Other are left out.
  • Change compares pooled earlier quarters (2025-Q3 and Q4) with pooled recent quarters (2026-Q2 to Q4), in percentage points. A move is clear when a two-proportion z-test gives p < 0.05. With 131 tests, six or seven clear results would be expected by chance alone, so 21 is a real signal, and any single borderline result deserves care.
  • 95% ranges use the standard error behind each test, so a range clears zero exactly when the move is clear.
  • Opportunity score: demand (share percentile, 30), momentum (change percentile, 35), evidence (1 − p, 15), room to enter (share of the dish’s venue mentions that go to one venue, doubled and capped, 20), minus 10 where guests also name it in “Bin it”. Weights are a judgement; the table view shows every component so you can re-weight in the CSV.
  • Segments come from each guest’s own description; group sizes are in the panel above.
  • Menu over time (chapters 5 to 7 and 14, food courses only): churn is half the sum of absolute changes in food-group share, earlier to recent, compared with 1,500 simulated reruns in which nothing changed and only sampling varied. Life-cycle stages use z-tests between quarters with 100+ answers (peaked: high in a middle quarter, clearly above the latest; recovering: the reverse) and a chi-square test for steadiness (p > 0.5, dish above the course median). Projections are a recency-weighted, damped trend with a planning range. Drink questions changed on 23 August 2026, so drinks are left out.
  • Drinks, restarted (chapter 8): built only from the soft and alcoholic drink questions asked since 23 August 2026. Answers that belong to the other question are left out. Shares carry 95% Wilson ranges; no movement is tested until two quarters each hold 100 answers.
  • How we compare (chapter 9): outside figures are quoted from their publishers with a link and date, and kept apart from Dream Menu data. They often measure something different (polls of all adults, delivery orders, menu listings, sales), so a difference is reported as a finding. Verdicts: agrees (same direction or a similar level), challenges (opposite direction or a clearly different level), context (a frame rather than a test).
  • Qualitative fieldwork, not a statistically representative survey of the population.
