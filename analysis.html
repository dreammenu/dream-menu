What the data says this quarter

Each line is written from the numbers and rechecked at every refresh. The sections below show the evidence.

Use it tobrief a team in two minutes. Every line is rechecked at each refresh, so quote it with its date.

Question one

2What do guests want?

Seafood to start, steak and beef for main, potatoes and fries on the side

Demand first. The chart shows what each course is made of, by food group. Below it is the life cycle that menu analysts use: a dish emerges, grows, becomes established and fades. 13 dishes are established staples that hold their share every quarter. Then supply: we read the published menus of 45 of the venues guests name most. Tiramisu is the dessert answer of 12.2% of guests but is listed on 23% of the 30 menus that print desserts, against 57% for other chocolate desserts, which 2.7% name.

Use it toset the shape of a menu or a range: lead each course with its biggest food group, protect the staples, and trial rising dishes as specials.

Question two

3What is changing?

Sides are being rewritten; the rest of the menu is holding

18.3% of side answers now sit in a different food group than in late 2025, against about 8.0% that sampling alone would produce. No other course clears that bar.

Use it toset priorities for the next menu change. Review sides first; change starters, mains and desserts for cost or quality, not to chase a trend.

Drinks: a new baseline since August 2026

The drinks question was split into soft and alcoholic on 23 August 2026, so this series starts there. Levels only for now; movement will be tested once two quarters each hold 100 answers.

Question three

4Where does it differ?

 

The national picture hides real differences between cities, and between what guests name and what guides reward. Shares, not counts, are used throughout, so a smaller city is compared fairly with Glasgow.

Use it tofit a menu to its market. Lean into dishes that sit above average in your city, and think twice before importing a favourite that sits below it.

Question four

5Can we trust it?

Published research backs Dream Menu on 13 of 21 topics; 6 more are local insights

Every finding is checked against wider hospitality research: YouGov and Drinkaware polling, trade bodies such as the SLTA and the British Soft Drinks Association, wholesaler studies from Bidfood and CGA by NIQ, Seafish and Seafood Scotland, and sales data. On 13 of 21 topics they point the same way. Where they differ, the reason is local: Scottish foodies and the trade see a shift before the national average does. Outside figures sit beside Dream Menu data with their source and date, never inside it. Partners see 12 more comparisons, on dish and drink movement and trading conditions.

Use it tocalibrate confidence. Where outside data agrees, act; where it differs, test first, because foodies tend to lead the wider public.

Question five

What should I do?

The same evidence, read for four kinds of reader

Each recommendation follows from the sections above and is rewritten when the numbers change.

Running a venue or a kitchen

Review the side list first: it is the only course that has moved beyond chance. Protect the staples (tiramisu, scallops and triple-cooked chips). Give alcohol-free its own priced section: 7.9% of guests choose it when asked for an alcoholic drink. On the dessert list, check the 3 most-named desserts first (tiramisu, sticky toffee pudding, baked cheesecake): together they are 28.5% of dessert answers, and each is on 23% or fewer of the menus we read.

Operator toolkit →

Opening a venue or investing

Guests broadly agree on what desserts should be, so compete there on quality, not novelty. Starters are the most open course: that is where a signature dish can stand out. Recommendation is concentrated (the top 10% of venues take 44% of mentions), so plan for word of mouth from day one. For pricing, the middle printed price at the 45 audited venues is £9.00 for a starter and £17.95 for a main (half of all mains fall between £15.50 and £23.42).

Venue map and rankings →

Supplying venues or building a range

Build the core range around the 13 steady staples and follow the shift within sides. Partners see which food groups gained and lost, a score for every dish and a three-quarter planning range. The menu audit shows where listings trail demand: tiramisu (12.2% of guests, 23% of menus); sticky toffee pudding (10.9% of guests, 23% of menus); baked cheesecake (5.4% of guests, 23% of menus).

Who answers, and how these numbers are made

Who answers

Share of the 1,250 guests who answered the dish questions, by how they describe themselves.

iHow to read this

Each bar is one group’s share of the panel. Groups come from how guests introduce themselves, so they are a guide, not a strict job title.

The panel leans towards people who work in or write about food, which is why it tends to spot trends before the wider public.

Where their venues are

Share of the 3,011 UK venue mentions, by nation. 15% of all 3,555 mentions are outside the UK or not placed in a nation.

iHow to read this

Each bar is a nation’s share of the venues guests named in the UK. It shows where the evidence is strongest: findings lean towards Scotland because most venues named are there.

Scotland95%
England5%
Northern Ireland<1%

Read it as: a large panel of food-led people, most of them in Scotland and many working in the trade. It leads the wider public on trends more than it mirrors it, which is why the “Can we trust it?” section sets the findings against national sources. Age, home city and how often guests eat out are not collected yet.

Supporting charts

How settled is each course? Share of answers in the top three food groups

Are guests converging on the same favourites? Top-five share by quarter
How concentrated are recommendations? Share of mentions held by the most-named venues
  • Shares are of answers coded to a category within each course. Answers coded as Other are left out.
  • Change compares pooled earlier quarters (2025-Q3 and Q4) with pooled recent quarters (2026-Q2 to Q4), in percentage points. A move is clear when a two-proportion z-test gives p < 0.05. With 131 tests, six or seven clear results would be expected by chance alone, so 21 is a real signal, and any single borderline result deserves care.
  • 95% ranges use the standard error behind each test, so a range clears zero exactly when the move is clear.
  • Opportunity score: demand (share percentile, 30), momentum (change percentile, 35), evidence (1 − p, 15), room to enter (share of the dish’s venue mentions that go to one venue, doubled and capped, 20), minus 10 where guests also name it in “Bin it”. Weights are a judgement; the table view shows every component so you can re-weight in the CSV.
  • Segments come from each guest’s own description; group sizes are in the panel above.
  • Menu over time (chapters 5 to 7 and 14, food courses only): churn is half the sum of absolute changes in food-group share, earlier to recent, compared with 1,500 simulated reruns in which nothing changed and only sampling varied. Life-cycle stages use z-tests between quarters with 100+ answers (peaked: high in a middle quarter, clearly above the latest; recovering: the reverse) and a chi-square test for steadiness (p > 0.5, dish above the course median). Projections are a recency-weighted, damped trend with a planning range. Drink questions changed on 23 August 2026, so drinks are left out.
  • Drinks, restarted (chapter 8): built only from the soft and alcoholic drink questions asked since 23 August 2026. Answers that belong to the other question are left out. Shares carry 95% Wilson ranges; no movement is tested until two quarters each hold 100 answers.
  • How we compare (chapter 9): outside figures are quoted from their publishers with a link and date, and kept apart from Dream Menu data. They often measure something different (polls of all adults, delivery orders, menu listings, sales), so a difference is reported as a finding. Verdicts: agrees (same direction or a similar level), challenges (opposite direction or a clearly different level), context (a frame rather than a test).
  • Qualitative fieldwork, not a statistically representative survey of the population.
